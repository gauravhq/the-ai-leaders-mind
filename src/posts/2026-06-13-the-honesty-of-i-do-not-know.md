---
title: "The Honesty of I Do Not Know"
date: 2026-06-13
pageDescription: "A tool rarely says it. A trustworthy leader has to."
---
A model almost never says it does not know. Asked a question, it produces an answer, fluent and complete, whether or not it has any basis for one. That single habit, the refusal to admit uncertainty, is one of the most important differences between the machine and a trustworthy person.

Because you work with a tool that never says I do not know, the burden of honesty about uncertainty falls entirely on you. If you also paper over what you are unsure of, then nobody in the decision is telling the truth about its shaky parts, and shaky parts are exactly where things break.

Saying I do not know is not weakness. It is the thing that tells people which of your statements to lean on and which to hold lightly. A leader who admits the edges of their knowledge is far more trustworthy than one who projects certainty everywhere, because the certainty everywhere is obviously false.

So model the honesty the machine cannot. Name what you are sure of and what you are not. In a world of tools that fake confidence perfectly, a person willing to say I do not know becomes rare, and rarer still, believed.
